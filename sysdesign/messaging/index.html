Messaging and event-driven design

System design home

Not everything has to happen while the user waits. Putting a message between a producer and a consumer decouples them in time (the consumer can be slow or down), in load (bursts are absorbed by the queue), and in knowledge (the producer does not need to know who consumes). Messaging is how large systems stay responsive and resilient, and how services stay independent. It also brings new problems: duplicates, ordering, failures that happen later, and consistency across services.

Synchronous (request/response)

The caller waits for the answer. Simple and immediate, but latency and failures add up along the chain, and a slow dependency slows everyone.

Asynchronous (messages/events)

The caller hands off work and continues. Absorbs spikes, isolates failures, enables fan-out; at the cost of eventual consistency and harder debugging.

Delivery guarantees and idempotency

  • At-most-once: fire and forget; messages may be lost (metrics sampling).
  • At-least-once: the broker redelivers until acknowledged; messages may arrive twice (the consumer crashed after processing but before acking). The practical default.
  • Exactly-once end to end is achieved by at-least-once delivery plus an idempotent consumer (or transactional processing inside one system, as in Kafka transactions).

Duplicates and the idempotent consumer

  • Poison messages that always fail are moved to a dead-letter queue after N attempts, so they do not block the queue; alert on DLQ depth.
  • Retries use exponential backoff with jitter; some brokers support scheduled redelivery.
  • Ordering: to keep one customer's events in order, route them to the same partition (partition key = customer id) and process each partition sequentially.
  • Large payloads: store them in object storage and send a reference (claim-check pattern).

Load levelling and competing consumers

A queue absorbs bursts so that consumers can work at their own pace. Add consumers (competing consumers) when the backlog grows; the queue length is the best autoscaling signal.

each consumer handles 25 msgs/s

If producers are permanently faster than consumers, no queue can save you: the backlog (and the age of the oldest message) grows without bound. Monitor queue age, not just length, and shed or throttle load when it keeps rising.

The transactional outbox

A service often must update its database and publish an event. Doing both directly is unsafe: the process can crash between them, leaving the database changed but no event sent (or the reverse). The outbox pattern writes the event into an outbox table in the same database transaction; a relay publishes it afterwards.

Sagas: transactions across services

A business transaction such as "place order" spans services (orders, payments, inventory, shipping), each with its own database. Instead of a distributed lock-step transaction, a saga runs a sequence of local transactions; if a step fails, it runs compensating actions to undo the earlier steps.

Orchestration

A central orchestrator (workflow engine) tells each service what to do and handles failures. Easy to follow and change; the orchestrator is a central piece. Durable Functions, Logic Apps, Step Functions, Temporal.

Choreography

Each service reacts to events and emits its own. No central coordinator and loosely coupled; harder to see the whole flow and to avoid cycles as it grows.

  • Change data capture (CDC): stream the database's own change log (Debezium, Cosmos DB change feed, DynamoDB Streams) into events, so the database stays the single source of truth.
  • Schemas: version events and keep them backward compatible (add fields, never repurpose them); a schema registry helps.
  • Observability: propagate a correlation/trace id in every message so a business flow can be followed across services.
